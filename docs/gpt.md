What if someone who has never been able to see a NASA image could still explore the data, discover something unexpected, and feel the scale of the planet through sound and touch?

Your core idea already lends itself to this: NASA imagery becomes sound, and the turntable gives people a way to explore it. The features should make blind and low-vision users explorers and interpreters of the data, not passive listeners to a description.

Feature ideas specifically for Bloop









1. “Explore the Earth with your ears”
Top priority
Make the turntable a real, nonvisual instrument. Users can move through the dataset using keyboard controls or screen-reader-accessible buttons, hearing how the sound changes as they move across the globe.
- Hear the current region and its coordinates.
- Move east, west, north, or south in predictable steps.
- Jump between meaningful regions, such as the Bay of Bengal or the Arctic.
- Ask for the exact data value at the current location.
Why it matters: They can choose where to investigate instead of having a sighted narrator decide what is worth describing.












2. “What changed?” — an auditory time machine
Top priority
Let users compare the same location or region across two dates. One sound represents the earlier measurement; another represents the later measurement. A spoken summary states the measured difference, units, and time period.

Example interaction:
- “Play July.”
- “Now play December.”
- “Compare the two.”
Bloop could say: “At this location, the measured sea-surface temperature decreased by X degrees Celsius between these dates,” but only when the underlying data actually supports that statement.

Why it matters: The user isn't just hearing an attractive soundtrack. They're discovering a scientific change and deciding what to examine next.












3. A tactile “record” that actually teaches geography
Distinctive extension
Instead of printing decorative vinyl grooves, create a raised-relief map or tactile circular map that represents a selected NASA dataset.
- Raised outlines help users orient themselves.
- Textures distinguish land, ocean, and data regions.
- A numbered or Braille-labelled legend explains the textures.
- A matching audio track guides the user through the physical map.
Start with a simple printable tactile graphic, if you can validate the design with users, rather than building custom hardware.

Why it matters: Touch gives spatial context that audio alone can struggle to convey. The map becomes something users can explore at their own pace.












4. A low-vision mode that is genuinely customizable
Don't assume every low-vision user wants a screen reader or a completely nonvisual interface.

Offer:
- Large, resizable text and numeric values.
- High-contrast map themes and clear boundaries.
- A magnified map window that follows the selected region.
- Adjustable playback speed and volume.
- Patterns or labels in addition to color.
Why it matters: Low vision varies widely. Some people use magnification, some use speech, and some switch between both. The interface should let them choose. 
W3C+1













5. “Tell me what I'm hearing”
Let users request three levels of explanation:
- Orientation: What dataset is this, and what does the sound represent?
- Interpretation: What pattern is audible in this section?
- Evidence: What are the exact value, units, location, date, source, and uncertainty?
Every claim should be tied to the actual data and mapping. If the sound represents relative temperature rather than literal temperature, Bloop must say so.

Why it matters: Users get the context to understand the sound, the freedom to interpret it, and a way to verify what they hear.












6. A “discover something yourself” challenge
Build short, optional investigations into the experience:
- “Find a region where the sound changes sharply.”
- “Compare the same region across two dates.”
- “Find the warmest location among these three sampled points.”
Users explore first; Bloop reveals the verified answer and evidence afterward. Never make success depend on hearing a particular pitch without training or on guessing what the designer intended.

Why it matters: It positions blind and low-vision users as people who can investigate, reason about, and learn science—not simply consume educational content.

One distinction: users who want actual Braille output would still need their own refreshable Braille display. But you can develop and demonstrate Bloop without owning one.
What Bloop can build entirely in software

1. Braille-compatible interface
Build this
Make all controls, values, locations, and navigation accessible to screen readers and refreshable Braille displays through semantic HTML and accessible labels.




2. Braille Data Explorer
Best differentiator
Users navigate a NASA dataset, hear its sonification, and inspect the exact value, units, location, date, and source in a text panel. A screen reader can read the panel, and a connected Braille display can present its text.




3. Low-vision mode
Build this
Add large text, high contrast, magnification, clear map boundaries, and adjustable sound. Users should be able to switch between visual and nonvisual exploration.




4. Accessible time comparison

Let users compare two dates using keyboard controls, audio, and exact spoken or displayed values. No additional hardware is needed.
